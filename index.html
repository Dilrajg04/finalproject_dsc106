Where do these numbers come from? Every value is output from five published climate models in CMIP6, the international model comparison used by the UN's IPCC climate reports. Years up to 2014 are the models' simulations of the past; later years are projections that assume a future emissions path. They show the expected long-term trend, not a forecast for any single year.

Data & transformations. Near-surface air temperature (tas, monthly) from five CMIP6 climate models, read from the public CMIP6 archive on Google Cloud. For each city we take the model grid cell nearest to it (about 100–250 km wide, so coastal cities partly include ocean). Monthly values are averaged into yearly values, then each model is compared with its own 1850–1900 average, so differences between models' absolute temperatures cancel out. Historical runs cover 1850–2014; scenario runs cover 2015–2100. The map averages the 20 years ending in the selected year and the 5 models together.

Prototype write-up

What have we done so far?

We decided on using the CMIP6 climate dataset to answer the question: “How does San Diego’s projected warming compare to major cities around the world?”. We then built a data pipeline that pulls monthly near-surface air temperatures from five climate models, (CESM2, GFDL-ESM4, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, UKESM1-0-LL and IPSL-CM6A-LR) respectively. We ran these models through 3 runs: the historical period from (1850 to 2014), the middle-road SSP2-4.5 and high emissions SSP5-8.5 (2015 to 2100). From these runs we extracted 24 major cities plus a global average and built a D3 page around visualizing them with a world map, a city ranking and an interactive guess to reveal a comparison chart. Additionally we added a timeline slider with a play button that animates rising temperatures across major cities worldwide from 1850 to 2100, alongside an animated cities ranked chart.

What will be the most challenging part of our project to design, and why?

Our biggest challenge would be the model grid size. Each cell spans roughly 100-250km and our question is based upon cities which may be smaller in area. San Diego’s cell for example includes part of the Pacific, which warms much slower than land, and some of the city’s “low warning” may come from the ocean rather than the city itself. The models also disagree, with Lagos ranging from +1.5 to +4.0 C and UKESM1 and CESM2 models run hot enough to push the global average up to +3.1 C, making it difficult to visualize without confusing readers. While running through the models we also had to adjust our narrative: our proposal said San Diego would warm the least, but the data now ranks it 4th lowest, which the story has to follow the data and on the technical side, non standard model calendars broke our data run during testing until we installed cftime.
